> **One idea worth keeping**
>
> Rather than asking whether carbohydrates, insulin, amino acids, or ketones are “good” or “bad” in isolation, Bikman’s framework encourages us to look at the whole system, distinguish building from preservation, and examine which mechanisms are actually responsible for the outcome we observe.

## The Idea

Ben Bikman’s central argument is that building or preserving muscle on a low-carbohydrate ketogenic diet is possible because insulin is only one part of the picture. He distinguishes between **anabolism**, the process of building tissue, and **catabolism**, the process of breaking it down. In his account, insulin is better understood as anti-catabolic than directly anabolic: it helps protect muscle protein from breakdown, but does not by itself drive muscle protein synthesis.

Bikman therefore shifts attention to amino acids, especially leucine and other branched-chain amino acids. These nutrients can directly stimulate muscle protein synthesis and activate the mTOR pathway, which acts as a major regulator of cellular growth. He argues that when insulin levels are relatively low, amino acids can **step in** and provide both a building signal and some protection against protein breakdown. In practical terms, his point is that low carbohydrate intake does not automatically imply an inability to maintain or gain muscle, provided protein intake and resistance training are adequate.

The second half of the lecture focuses on ketones, particularly beta-hydroxybutyrate, or BHB. Bikman rejects the idea that ketones are merely an emergency backup fuel. He argues that the brain readily uses them when they are available, reducing its demand for glucose. According to his explanation, this may help preserve muscle because the body has less need to convert amino acids into glucose during prolonged carbohydrate restriction. He also cites experimental work suggesting that BHB may influence muscle directly by supporting mitochondrial function, reducing oxidative stress, and limiting muscle loss under conditions such as aging, disuse, inflammation, or heavy training.

Bikman also challenges several common assumptions. He argues that short-term reductions in fat-free mass on ketogenic diets may partly reflect water loss rather than actual muscle loss, and he questions the claim that high insulin is required for muscle growth. He further points to research on keto-adapted athletes suggesting that muscle glycogen can be maintained and replenished even when carbohydrate intake is low.

The evidence he discusses spans **cell, animal, and human studies**, so not every result carries the same weight. But the broader methodological lesson is valuable beyond nutrition: complex outcomes rarely have a single cause. A useful explanation should separate mechanisms, examine compensatory pathways, and avoid mistaking one visible factor for the whole system.

## Language Worth Keeping

### **not the full picture**
An explanation can be partly true and still incomplete.

> Talent matters, but it is **not the full picture** when we explain long-term achievement.

### **a bit of an edge**
A small practical or competitive advantage.

> Better sleep may give you **a bit of an edge** when performance matters.

### **to put a fine point on it**
A clean way to make an argument more precise.

> **To put a fine point on it**, the question is not whether insulin matters, but what role it actually plays.

### **flip the switch**
To trigger or activate a process.

> Leucine helps **flip the switch** for muscle protein synthesis.

### **step in**
To take over or compensate when another factor is absent or insufficient.

> When motivation disappears, routine has to **step in**.

### **doesn’t hold water**
An argument cannot survive closer scrutiny.

> The claim sounds intuitive, but it **doesn’t hold water** once the mechanism is examined.

## My Reflection

In the past, I often made purchasing decisions on impulse. To put a fine point on it, I would buy things the moment I wanted them, without considering the full picture. As a result, I wasted money and regretted many purchases afterwards.

My older sister eventually pointed out the mechanism behind this pattern: desire acted like a trigger that flipped the switch from wanting to buying. Her explanation held water, so I changed the rule. Whenever I want something now, I add it to my cart and wait until the next day before deciding. That short delay gives reflection time to step in.

It has saved me a surprising amount of money, and it reminds me that a small change in behaviour can go a long way.
